# Bradley-Terry Assumption Failure in Preference Heads

> **Topic:** Loss Functions & Output Heads · **ID:** `08-loss-and-heads/bradley-terry-failure-preference-heads` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement

Nearly every reward model and every direct-alignment loss (DPO, RRHF, SimPO) assumes human pairwise preferences are generated by a **scalar latent utility** under the Bradley–Terry (BT) link. That assumption forces transitivity and a single total order over responses. Human preference data is aggregated over heterogeneous annotators with conflicting criteria, which is exactly the regime where Arrow-style cycles appear.

Three distinct problems get conflated:

- **Measurement.** Given a prompt $x$ and a response set, is the population preference matrix $P(x)$ actually BT-representable? Estimate the *degree* of violation with enough votes per pair to separate genuine intransitivity from sampling noise.
- **Method.** If $P$ is not BT-representable, does a head with more capacity (skew-symmetric bilinear, multi-dimensional, or a full preference function $p_\theta(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)$) produce better *policies*, not just better held-out pairwise accuracy?
- **Theory.** Under a known nonzero cyclic component, what does BT-MLE converge to, and how much regret does the resulting policy incur against the Nash equilibrium of the preference game?

Solving it means: a calibrated estimate of the cyclic mass in real preference data, plus a demonstration that removing the BT constraint changes downstream policy quality by more than annotator-noise error bars.

## 2. Formal Setting

Prompt $x \sim \mathcal{D}$, responses $y \in \mathcal{Y}$. The measurable object is the **population preference probability**

$$p^\star(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mathcal{A}}\big[\Pr(a \text{ picks } y_1)\big],$$

the expectation taken over the annotator population $\mathcal{A}$. It is measured as $\hat p_{ij} = k_{ij}/n_{ij}$ from $n_{ij}$ independent votes, with standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n_{ij}}$. In practice $n_{ij}=1$ for essentially all RLHF corpora — this is the root measurement problem.

**BT head.** A scalar head $r_\theta: \mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}$ with

$$p_\theta(y_1 \succ y_2\mid x) = \sigma\big(r_\theta(x,y_1) - r_\theta(x,y_2)\big),\qquad \mathcal{L} = -\mathbb{E}\log \sigma(r_\theta(x,y_w)-r_\theta(x,y_l)).$$

$r_\theta$ is identified only up to an additive constant per prompt.

**Transitivity ladder.** Write $p_{ij}=p^\star(y_i\succ y_j)$.
- Weak stochastic transitivity (WST): $p_{ij}\ge\tfrac12 \wedge p_{jk}\ge\tfrac12 \Rightarrow p_{ik}\ge\tfrac12$.
- Strong stochastic transitivity (SST): same antecedent $\Rightarrow p_{ik}\ge\max(p_{ij},p_{jk})$.
- BT $\subsetneq$ SST $\subsetneq$ WST. BT additionally requires the **linear-ordinal** condition $\sigma^{-1}(p_{ik}) = \sigma^{-1}(p_{ij}) + \sigma^{-1}(p_{jk})$ for all triples.

**Cyclic mass.** Decompose the logit matrix $L_{ij}=\sigma^{-1}(p_{ij})$ (skew-symmetric) by the combinatorial Hodge decomposition into a gradient (transitive) part and a curl (cyclic) part:

$$L = \underbrace{\mathrm{grad}\,r}_{\text{BT-representable}} \oplus\; L^{\mathrm{cyc}},\qquad \kappa = \frac{\|L^{\mathrm{cyc}}\|_F^2}{\|L\|_F^2} \in [0,1].$$

$\kappa$ is the single scalar the measurement problem must return, with a confidence interval, debiased for the $O(1/n_{ij})$ inflation from finite votes.

**Assumptions known to be violated.** (i) A single latent utility exists — false whenever annotators weight helpfulness and harmlessness differently. (ii) Votes are i.i.d. given $(x,y_1,y_2)$ — false; annotator identity is a strong random effect. (iii) Position/length effects are absent — false; length bias alone shifts $p$ by several points. (iv) $\sigma^{-1}$ additivity — untested at scale, because $n_{ij}=1$ makes $\hat L$ infinitely noisy per entry.

## 3. State of the Art

**Theory SOTA (established).** Shah, Balakrishnan, Guntuboyina and Wainwright's analysis of the SST class (ICML 2016 / *IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory* 2017) gives minimax rates for estimating $P$ under SST without a parametric link, and exhibits a gap between the information-theoretic rate and the best known polynomial-time estimator. Shah et al. (*JMLR* 2016) give topology-dependent minimax bounds for BT/Thurstone estimation. Munos et al., *Nash Learning from Human Feedback* (ICML 2024), drop the reward entirely: the target is the Nash equilibrium of the two-player preference game, which exists for any $p^\star$, transitive or not. Swamy et al., *A Minimaximalist Approach to RLHF* (SPO, ICML 2024), show a single-player self-play scheme converges to the minimax winner without a BT assumption. Azar et al. ($\Psi$PO/IPO, AISTATS 2024) show the failure is not only cyclic: under near-deterministic preferences the BT link makes the KL regulariser vacuous, and IPO fixes this by replacing $\sigma^{-1}$ with identity.

**Empirical SOTA (claimed, partly unablated).** Zhang et al., *General Preference Modeling with Preference Representations for Aligning Language Models* (2024), replace the scalar with latent vectors under a skew-symmetric bilinear form, which can express cycles in $O(d)$ dimensions, and report RewardBench gains. Wang et al., *Interpretable Preferences via Multi-Objective Reward Modeling* (ArmoRM, EMNLP 2024), use a multi-objective head with a gating mixture. Both report **benchmark numbers only**: neither ablates against a matched-capacity BT head on a dataset with measured $\kappa$, so the gain is not attributable to cycle expressivity. Sun et al., *Rethinking Bradley–Terry Models in Preference-Based Reward Modeling* (2024/2025), argue BT's practical value in RLHF is as an order-consistent classifier rather than a correct generative model, and give alternatives with comparable performance.

**Deployment SOTA.** Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., ICML 2024) fits BT to millions of votes and publishes the fitted scores as the field's leaderboard, with bootstrap CIs but no published $\kappa$.

## 4. What Is Known

- **Inter-annotator agreement is low.** InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., NeurIPS 2022) reports $\approx 73\%$ agreement among training labelers and $\approx 77\%$ between researchers and labelers, at 40 annotators and ~33k comparisons. Anthropic's HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) reports similar. This caps any preference model's achievable accuracy near $\sim 75\%$.
- **Reward-model accuracy plateaus.** Held-out pairwise accuracy of large reward models sits in the 65–75% band on in-distribution human data; RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024) scores above 90% only on subsets with synthetic, near-deterministic gold labels.
- **Ranking is not learned even where BT holds.** Chen et al., *Preference Learning Algorithms Do Not Learn Preference Rankings* (NeurIPS 2024), find DPO/RLHF-tuned models rank the preferred completion above the dispreferred one on under $60\%$ of held-out training pairs, at 7B–70B scale — an *optimisation* gap that sits on top of the misspecification gap and confounds it.
- **Cycles are real in game-like domains.** Balduzzi et al., *Re-evaluating Evaluation* (NeurIPS 2018), show real agent populations have large cyclic components. Bertrand et al. (AISTATS 2023) show Elo's additivity fails on real-world game data.
- **BT is the *unique* link consistent with independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives** (Luce's choice axiom, 1959) — so any measured IIA violation is a direct BT falsification.

## 5. What Is Not Known

- **Methodologically blocked.** $\kappa$ has never been measured on an LLM preference corpus, because standard corpora have $n_{ij}=1$. With one vote per pair, the cyclic and noise components are not separable; there is no accepted debiased estimator of $\kappa$ under annotator random effects. This is the binding gap.
- **Empirically open.** Whether a cycle-expressive head yields better *policies* (win rate against a strong judge, best-of-$n$ quality) rather than better pairwise accuracy. The experiment is runnable at 7B for well under $50$k; nobody has run it with a matched-capacity control.
- **Theoretically open.** No characterisation of the policy regret of BT-MLE as a function of $\kappa$ under KL-regularised RL — i.e. no bound of the form $\mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{\mathrm{BT}}) \le f(\kappa,\beta)$ against the Nash policy. Also open: sample complexity of NLHF-style equilibrium computation relative to BT reward learning.

## 6. Why It Is Hard

**The obstruction is non-identifiability under $n_{ij}=1$ sampling, compounded by a loss that cannot see the defect.** Two things are entangled:

1. With one vote per pair, $\hat p_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$. Every observed triple is trivially a "cycle" or "not" at random; the cyclic component of $\hat L$ is dominated by Bernoulli noise, which contributes $O(1)$ curl. Separating signal requires $n_{ij}\gtrsim 100$ per pair, a different data collection regime, not a different estimator.
2. Validation log-loss — the number practitioners actually monitor — is nearly insensitive to misspecification. As Section 10 shows, a maximally cyclic triple costs the BT head about $0.04$ nats per comparison, while annotator-noise-driven variation across data slices is an order of magnitude larger. The evaluation does not measure the thing it names.

A secondary obstruction: the optimisation gap (Chen et al. 2024) is larger than the misspecification gap, so any observed improvement from a richer head can be explained by easier optimisation unless capacity and step count are matched.

## 7. Current Research (as of 2026)

- **Game-theoretic alignment.** NLHF (Munos et al., Google DeepMind), SPO (Swamy et al., CMU), and online-DPO/IPO variants target minimax winners rather than BT rewards. Adoption in production post-training pipelines remains partial *(frontier — verify)*.
- **Structured heads.** Skew-symmetric / multi-dimensional preference representations (Zhang et al.), multi-objective and mixture-of-judges heads (ArmoRM; industrial post-training reports). Open question is attribution of gains.
- **Distributional and annotator-aware modelling.** Explicit annotator random effects and personalised reward models; distributional reward models that predict a vote distribution instead of a point score *(frontier — verify)*.
- **Arena methodology.** Repeated-comparison sub-studies and transitivity audits of leaderboard data *(frontier — verify — no published $\kappa$ for Chatbot Arena as of this writing)*.

## 8. Concrete Next Experiment

**Question.** Is the cyclic mass $\kappa$ in real LLM preference data large enough to change policies?

**Scale.** 300 prompts stratified across helpfulness / safety / style-conflict. Per prompt, 6 responses from 6 distinct models $\Rightarrow$ 15 pairs, 4,500 pairs total. Collect $n_{ij}=100$ independent votes per pair from a pool of $\ge 50$ annotators, randomised for position and annotator: **450,000 votes** (~$0.10–0.15/vote $\Rightarrow$ $45$k–$70$k). At $n=100$, SE on $\hat p$ is $\le 0.05$, enough to certify $p_{ij}>0.5$ at $p=0.60$ with 95% power.

**Arms.**
- *Control:* scalar BT head, 7B backbone, trained on the 450k votes.
- *Treatment:* skew-symmetric bilinear head with $d=8$, identical backbone, identical parameter count in the head-adjacent layers, identical optimiser and steps.
- *Second control:* BT head with $d=8$ worth of extra MLP width and no cycle expressivity — isolates capacity from structure.

**Deciding number.** Held-out pairwise accuracy of the treatment minus the *capacity-matched* control, restricted to pairs drawn from triples with a statistically certified SST violation (bootstrap, 95%, Holm-corrected). Pre-register a threshold of **+3.0 accuracy points**. Report $\hat\kappa$ with its bootstrap CI alongside; $\hat\kappa < 0.02$ with the accuracy delta under 1 point would close the measurement variant as "BT is adequate for LLM preference data".

## 10. Worked Example

One prompt, three responses: A (concise, mildly unsafe), B (safe, verbose), C (helpful, medium). With $n=100$ votes per pair, suppose

$$\hat p_{AB}=0.65,\quad \hat p_{BC}=0.65,\quad \hat p_{CA}=0.60 .$$

Each is more than 2 SE above $0.5$ ($\mathrm{SE}\approx0.048$), so the cycle is certified. WST is violated.

**What BT must predict.** $\sigma^{-1}(0.65)=0.619$, so $r_A-r_B=0.619$ and $r_B-r_C=0.619$, hence $r_A-r_C=1.238$ and $p_{CA}=\sigma(-1.238)=0.225$ — against an observed $0.60$. The BT family cannot come near.

**What BT actually fits.** By the cyclic symmetry, the MLE collapses toward $r_A\approx r_B\approx r_C$, predicting $p=0.5$ everywhere.

**The cost the loss reports.** Per-comparison log-loss under $p=0.5$ is $\log 2 = 0.693$ nats. The irreducible entropy of the data is

$$\tfrac13\big[2H(0.65)+H(0.60)\big] = \tfrac13[2(0.647)+0.673] = 0.656 \text{ nats}.$$

**Excess loss from total BT failure: 0.037 nats, or 5.3%.** Typical prompt-slice variation in validation loss on real RLHF data exceeds this. So a practitioner watching validation loss sees nothing.

**The cost the policy pays.** The fitted head is indifferent among A, B, C. Best-of-3 sampling picks uniformly, yielding expected win rate $0.5$ against any of them. The Nash equilibrium of this cycle is the mixed strategy $\pi^\star$ over $\{A,B,C\}$ that is unexploitable by construction; a *deterministic* argmax policy induced by any tie-break is beaten $60$–$65\%$ of the time by one of the other two. The head's loss is 5% off; the policy's exploitability is 15 points off. That divergence — a loss that barely moves while the induced policy becomes arbitrary — is the obstruction.

**Cost of even seeing this.** Certifying $p_{CA}=0.60 > 0.5$ at 95% confidence needs $n\ge(1.96\cdot 0.49/0.10)^2\approx 92$ votes for that single pair. Every public RLHF corpus provides one.
