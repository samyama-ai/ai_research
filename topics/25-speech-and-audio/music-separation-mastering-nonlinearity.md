# Music Source Separation Ceiling from Mastering Nonlinearity

> **Topic:** Speech & Audio · **ID:** `25-speech-and-audio/music-separation-mastering-nonlinearity` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement

Music source separation (MSS) is trained and scored on the assumption that a mix is a **linear sum of stems**. MUSDB18 mixes are literally built that way. Commercial releases are not: a mastering chain applies bus compression, brickwall limiting, saturation, dynamic EQ and stereo processing to the *sum*, after the stems exist. The mix is $x = \mathcal{M}(\sum_s y_s)$ with $\mathcal{M}$ nonlinear and time-varying.

Three variants, usually conflated:

- **Measurement.** For a mastered mix, what *is* the target for "vocals"? The pre-master stem $y_{\text{voc}}$ cannot be an additive component of $x$. Any SDR reported against it charges the model for the mastering engineer's work. **This variant is not well posed today.**
- **Method.** Given a well-posed target, how much of the SOTA gap on loud commercial music is recoverable by training-time augmentation (limiter simulation) versus architecture?
- **Theory.** For a known $\mathcal{M}$, is the decomposition of $\mathcal{M}(\sum_s y_s)$ into per-source contributions identifiable at all? Harmonic distortion products generated by intermodulation of two sources belong to neither.

Solving it means: a target definition for mastered audio that is (a) additive, so $\sum_s \tilde y_s = x$ exactly, (b) invariant to which *other* sources are present, and (c) a demonstration that measured SDR under that definition tracks listener-judged separation quality on real masters.

## 2. Formal Setting

Let $y_s \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times N}$, $s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$, be pre-master stereo stems at 44.1 kHz. The premix is $u = \sum_s y_s$. The master is $x = \mathcal{M}(u)$, where $\mathcal{M}$ is causal, nonlinear, with memory (attack/release time constants of order 1–100 ms).

**Gain-sharing target (measurable).** For any $\mathcal{M}$ decomposable as a sample-wise gain, define
$$g[n] = \frac{\mathcal{M}(u)[n]}{u[n]}, \qquad \tilde y_s[n] = g[n]\, y_s[n],$$
so $\sum_s \tilde y_s = x$ by construction. Measured by rendering the same chain twice — once on $u$, once on a null signal — or by logging the compressor's gain-reduction envelope from the plugin host. This is exactly the construction behind sample-wise gain inversion for de-limiting (Jeon & Lee, WASPAA 2023).

**Residual (the part that defeats gain-sharing).**
$$r = x - \sum_s g \odot y_s$$
is identically zero for a pure gain, nonzero for saturation, clipping, dynamic EQ, or mid/side processing. Measure $\rho = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\|x\|^2/\|r\|^2\right)$ in dB: the *linearizability* of the chain. $\rho \to \infty$ means gain-sharing is exact.

**Metrics as actually computed.** `museval` chunk-SDR (cSDR): median over 1 s windows and tracks of
$$\mathrm{SDR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\|y_{\text{target}}\|^2}{\|\hat y - y_{\text{target}}\|^2}$$
after BSS Eval's 512-tap distortion filters (Vincent et al., 2006). Utterance-SDR (uSDR), used by the Music Demixing Challenge, is the global-window variant without filters. SI-SDR (Le Roux et al., ICASSP 2019) optimizes a single scale $\alpha=\langle\hat y,y\rangle/\|y\|^2$.

**Loudness.** Integrated loudness $L$ in LUFS per ITU-R BS.1770-4. Gain reduction $\mathrm{GR}[n] = -20\log_{10} g[n]$; report its mean and 95th percentile.

**Assumptions and their status:**

| Assumption | Status |
|---|---|
| $x = \sum_s y_s$ | **Violated** for every commercial master; holds by construction in MUSDB18 |
| $\mathcal{M}$ is sample-wise gain | Approximately true for a clean limiter; **violated** by saturation, clippers, multiband, M/S |
| Target is a property of the source alone | **Violated**: $\tilde y_s$ depends on all other stems through $g$ |
| BSS Eval's 512-tap filter absorbs residual mismatch | Untested for time-varying gain; the filter is time-invariant per window |

## 3. State of the Art

**Empirical SOTA (linear-mixture benchmark).** Band-Split RoPE Transformer (Lu, Wang, Won, Nakamura, et al., ICASSP 2024) reports ~12.97 dB average cSDR on MUSDB18 test with large internal training data; Mel-RoFormer (Wang et al., 2024) is comparable. HT Demucs (Défossez, ICASSP 2023) reports 9.20 dB average with extra data, 7.52 dB MUSDB-only. BSRNN (Luo & Yu, TASLP 2023) reports 10.01 dB vocals with extra data. These are **benchmark numbers on linearly summed mixes**; none is an ablation of mastering robustness.

**Established for this problem.** LimitAug (Jeon & Lee, ISMIR 2022) — applying a limiter during training-data mixing so the training loudness distribution matches loud commercial music — is the one directly targeted method with a controlled comparison, and it improves separation on a loud commercial test set without hurting MUSDB18. De-limiter networks (Jeon & Lee, WASPAA 2023) recover the pre-limiter signal by estimating $g[n]$ and inverting it sample-wise; this is the operational realization of the gain-sharing decomposition.

**Claimed but unablated.** That RoFormer-class gains transfer to mastered audio. Public checkpoints are used commercially on real releases, and the resulting stems are widely judged usable, but no published evaluation separates "the model is robust" from "the metric never charged it for mastering". Effects normalization in mixing-style transfer (Martínez-Ramírez et al., ISMIR 2022; Koo et al., ICASSP 2023) makes the same domain-gap argument for mixing, not separation.

## 4. What Is Known

- **The linear-mixture benchmark is exhausted at a rate faster than perceptual gains.** MUSDB18 average cSDR moved ~5.3 dB (Open-Unmix, JOSS 2019) → ~7.7 dB (Hybrid Demucs, MDX 2021 winner) → ~13 dB (BS-RoFormer, 2024) on 50 test tracks. Scale: 150 tracks total, ~10 h audio.
- **Loudness domain gap is real and measurable.** MUSDB18-HQ mixes sit near −14 LUFS integrated; contemporary pop/EDM masters cluster near −9 to −8 LUFS. That is 5–6 dB of extra gain reduction the model never saw in training. Jeon & Lee (ISMIR 2022) built a loud-commercial test set and showed models trained on MUSDB-loudness data degrade on it; LimitAug closes part of the gap.
- **Sample-wise gain inversion works.** De-limiter networks (WASPAA 2023) recover pre-limiter audio at high fidelity, which confirms that for typical single-band limiting the residual $r$ is small — the chain is close to a pure time-varying gain.
- **Label/mixing noise materially changes rankings.** The SDX23 Label Noise leaderboard (Fabbro et al., TMLR 2024) showed that models robust to corrupted stem labels differ from clean-data winners — evidence that benchmark rank order is fragile to mixing-process assumptions.
- **SDR is a poor perceptual proxy at high values.** Established since SiSEC 2018 (Stöter, Liutkus, Ito, LVA/ICA 2018): listener MUSHRA scores and SDR correlate only loosely across systems.

## 5. What Is Not Known

- **Methodologically blocked.** There is no agreed target for a mastered mix. Until $\tilde y_s$ (or an alternative) is standardized, every number reported "on commercial music" mixes model error with target-definition error. This is the primary blocker.
- **Empirically open.** The size of the ceiling. Nobody has published the oracle experiment: the ideal-ratio-mask cSDR on the *same* stems, mixed linearly versus through a documented mastering chain, against both target definitions. It needs 50 tracks and a few GPU-hours — no training. The absence is one of convention, not cost.
- **Theoretically open.** Identifiability of source contributions under memoryless nonlinearity. For $x = \phi(y_1+y_2)$ with $\phi$ a known odd polynomial, the intermodulation terms $y_1^2 y_2$ have no source attribution under any additive decomposition. No published result gives conditions on $\phi$ and on source spectra under which a unique additive decomposition of $x$ exists.

## 6. Why It Is Hard

**Absent ground truth, plus an evaluation that does not measure what it names.** For a real commercial track, no version of "the vocals as they exist in the master" was ever rendered — the mastering engineer processed the two-track. So the field evaluates on synthetic linear mixes, where the metric is well defined and the assumption it encodes is exactly the one that fails in deployment. The failure is silent: a model can score 13 dB on MUSDB18 and be charged nothing for behavior under 6 dB of program-dependent gain reduction.

**Non-identifiability compounds it.** Even with full access to the chain, the gain-sharing target $\tilde y_s = g \odot y_s$ is *not a function of $y_s$ alone*. The same vocal take, placed in a denser arrangement, gets a different target — because $g$ responds to the drums. A model cannot be simultaneously correct on both without inferring the arrangement. And the saturation residual $r$ is unattributable in principle, not just unmeasured.

## 7. Current Research (as of 2026)

- **Limiter-aware training.** LimitAug-style augmentation and loudness-matched data pipelines (Jeon, Lee; Music and Audio Research Group, Seoul National University). Now standard in some open MSS training recipes *(frontier — verify adoption breadth)*.
- **Effect inversion as preprocessing.** De-limiting and distortion removal (Imort, Fabbro, Martínez-Ramírez, Uhlich, Koyama, Mitsufuji, ISMIR 2022; Sony AI / Sony CSL) used as a front-end before separation. Whether the two-stage pipeline beats end-to-end training on mastered mixes is unresolved.
- **Differentiable effect chains.** DeepAFx-ST (Steinmetz, Bryan, Reiss, 2022) and differentiable mixing consoles make $\mathcal{M}$ trainable, which in principle allows joint estimation of $g$ and the stems.
- **Richer stem datasets.** MoisesDB (Pereira et al., ISMIR 2023) — 240 tracks with a deeper stem taxonomy — gives the raw material for a paired mastered/unmastered corpus, though the mastering arm still has to be synthesized.
- **Benchmark reform.** Successors to SDX23 continue to add non-ideal-mixture tracks (label noise, bleeding) *(frontier — verify current edition)*.

## 8. Concrete Next Experiment

**The paired-master oracle ablation.** No training required.

- **Scale.** 50 MUSDB18-HQ test tracks (or 60 MoisesDB tracks held out), 4 stems, full length. Two mixes per track: **A** = linear sum, loudness-normalized to −14 LUFS; **B** = same sum through one fixed, published limiter chain, driven to −9 LUFS integrated. Log $g[n]$ during rendering. Report mean and p95 gain reduction. Compute $\rho$ to confirm the chain is near-gain-only ($\rho > 30$ dB).
- **Arms.** (1) **Oracle IRM** — the ideal ratio mask computed from true stems, the metric's own ceiling. (2) Frozen public BS-RoFormer checkpoint. (3) Same checkpoint after LimitAug-style fine-tuning on mastered mixes. Each scored on B against **T1** = pre-master stems $y_s$ and **T2** = gain-shared stems $g\odot y_s$.
- **Deciding number.** $\Delta_{\text{oracle}} = \mathrm{cSDR}_{\text{IRM}}(A) - \mathrm{cSDR}_{\text{IRM}}(B, \text{T2})$.
  - $\Delta_{\text{oracle}} < 0.5$ dB and model drop $> 2$ dB → the ceiling is a **method** problem; augmentation is the fix, benchmark stays valid under T2.
  - $\Delta_{\text{oracle}} > 2$ dB → the ceiling is in the signal and the metric; the field needs a new target, not a new architecture.
- **Secondary number.** $\mathrm{cSDR}(B,\text{T1}) - \mathrm{cSDR}(B,\text{T2})$ for the oracle. This is the SDR the community currently hands the mastering engineer's bill for. Predicted 5–10 dB (see §10).

Cost: ~3 GPU-hours inference plus rendering. It is unrun, not unrunnable.

## 10. Worked Example

One track, one limiter, showing why the current number is not about the model.

Take a 4-stem mix summed to a peak-normalized premix $u$. Push it through a brickwall limiter to −9 LUFS. Log the gain envelope $g[n]$: mean gain $\bar g = 0.83$ (−1.6 dB average gain reduction), p95 gain reduction 6 dB, so $g$ ranges roughly $[0.50, 1.00]$.

**The bill charged to the model under target T1 (pre-master stem).** Suppose the model is *perfect* under T2 — it outputs $\hat y_s = g\odot y_s$ exactly. Score it against $y_s$ with a free global scale (SI-SDR). Write $g = \bar g + \delta$, and treat $\delta$ as uncorrelated with $y_s$ with rms $\sigma_\delta$:
$$\mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR}(\hat y_s, y_s) \;=\; 10\log_{10}\frac{\bar g^{2}}{\sigma_\delta^{2}}.$$

| Limiting | $\bar g$ | $\sigma_\delta$ | Ceiling on a *perfect* separator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light (−1 dB avg GR) | 0.89 | 0.05 | **25.0 dB** |
| Typical pop (−1.6 dB) | 0.83 | 0.08 | **20.3 dB** |
| Loudness-war (−4 dB) | 0.63 | 0.20 | **10.0 dB** |
| Heavy EDM master | 0.55 | 0.28 | **5.9 dB** |

**The obstruction, visible.** SOTA vocals cSDR on MUSDB18 is ~13 dB. In the bottom two rows, the *target definition alone* caps a flawless separator at 10.0 and 5.9 dB. A model scoring 8 dB on a loudness-war master is not necessarily worse than one scoring 13 dB on MUSDB18 — it may be closer to its own ceiling. The two numbers are not comparable, and no published evaluation reports which regime its test tracks are in.

Second, the invariance failure. Keep the vocal $y_{\text{voc}}$ byte-identical and replace the sparse drum stem with a dense one. $g[n]$ changes; $\tilde y_{\text{voc}} = g\odot y_{\text{voc}}$ changes. Measured against the first arrangement's target, the *correct* answer for the second scores finitely — in the typical-pop row, around 20 dB. The target is a property of the mix, not the source. That is the non-identifiability, and it is why §8's oracle arm, not a bigger transformer, is the next move.
