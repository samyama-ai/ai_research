# Formal Grammar Coverage Limits for Program Synthesis Data

> **Topic:** Synthetic Data · **ID:** `30-synthetic-data/program-synthesis-grammar-coverage` · **Status:** open

## 1. Problem Statement

Synthetic training data for program synthesis is generated by sampling derivation trees from a formal grammar (a DSL, a subset of Python, a SyGuS grammar) and pairing each program with input/output examples or a natural-language description. The generator is cheap and unbounded: it can emit $10^{12}$ programs. The trained model is not unbounded — it saturates.

The problem: **given a grammar $G$ and a compute budget, what fraction of $G$'s expressive power can a model trained on samples from $G$ actually acquire, and which coverage functional over $G$ predicts downstream solve rate?**

Three variants, different difficulties:

- **Measurement.** Define a coverage statistic $C(D)$ of a synthetic corpus $D \subset L(G)$ that is (a) computable in time linear in $|D|$ and (b) monotonically related to held-out solve rate on a *target* program distribution $\mathcal{D}^*$. Open — no such statistic is validated.
- **Method.** Build a sampler $q$ over derivation trees that maximises solve rate per training token, beating uniform-by-size and beating hand-tuned heuristics. Partially solved for DSLs of $\le 40$ productions; unsolved for general-purpose languages.
- **Theory.** Prove a bound of the form: any learner trained on $m$ i.i.d. samples from *any* distribution supported on $L(G)$ fails on a $\ge \epsilon$ fraction of $\mathcal{D}^*$ whenever $\mathcal{D}^* \not\subseteq L(G)$, or when the semantic-to-syntactic collapse ratio of $G$ exceeds a threshold. Open.

Solving it means: a number computable from the generator config, before training, that predicts the ceiling.

## 2. Formal Setting

Let $G=(V,\Sigma,R,S)$ be a context-free grammar, $\mathcal{T}(G)$ its set of derivation trees, and $|t|$ the node count of $t \in \mathcal{T}(G)$. Write $\mathcal{T}_{\le n}$ for trees of size $\le n$.

**Enumeration.** With generating function $T(z)=\sum_n t_n z^n$ obtained from $R$ by the symbolic method, an unambiguous $G$ gives $t_n \sim c\,\alpha^{n} n^{-3/2}$ (Flajolet–Sedgewick). $\alpha$ is measured by fitting $\log t_n$ against $n$ from an exact DP count — no sampling needed.

**Syntactic coverage.** For a corpus $D$:

$$C_1(D) = \frac{|\{r \in R : r \text{ fires in some } t \in D\}|}{|R|}, \qquad C_k(D) = \frac{|\{\kappa \in \mathcal{C}_k(D)\}|}{|\mathcal{C}_k(G)|}$$

where a $k$-context $\kappa$ is a production together with its $k$ nearest ancestor productions and the child slot indices. $|\mathcal{C}_k(G)| \le |R|^{k+1}$; measured by hashing the ancestor path during generation.

**Semantic coverage.** Fix a finite input battery $I$. Two programs are observationally equivalent if they agree on all of $I$. Then

$$C_{\mathrm{sem}}(D) = \frac{|\{\,\llbracket t \rrbracket_I : t \in D\,\}|}{|\{\,\llbracket t \rrbracket_I : t \in \mathcal{T}_{\le n}\,\}|}$$

measured by executing every program on $I$ and hashing the output vector. Cost: $|D|\cdot|I|$ executions.

**Objective.** Model $M_\theta$ trained on $D \sim q^m$ under budget $B$ FLOPs. Downstream metric is solve rate $\pi = \Pr_{p \sim \mathcal{D}^*}[M_\theta \text{ synthesises } p' \text{ with } \llbracket p'\rrbracket_I = \llbracket p \rrbracket_I]$ at a fixed sample budget $k$ (pass@$k$). The **coverage ceiling** is $\pi^\star(G,B) = \sup_q \pi$, and the **grammar gap** is $\pi^\star(G,B) - \pi_{\mathcal{D}^*}$, where $\pi_{\mathcal{D}^*}$ uses real programs.

**Assumptions, and which fail.**

- *$\mathcal{D}^*$ is supported inside $L(G)$.* Violated for every general-purpose language: real code calls libraries and uses idioms with no production in the sampling grammar.
- *Uniform-by-size is neutral.* Violated — human code is far lower-entropy than the grammar allows (Hindle et al., ICSE 2012), so uniform sampling is itself a heavy distribution shift.
- *$I$ determines semantics.* Violated: observational equivalence on finite $I$ is a strict over-approximation; true equivalence is undecidable.
- *Samples are i.i.d.* Violated by dedup and rejection filters, which are non-i.i.d. and unmodelled in the reported coverage numbers.

## 3. State of the Art

**Established (grammar-based test generation).**

- Purdom (BIT, 1972): an algorithm producing a sentence set covering every production, with size $O(|R|)$. Production coverage is achievable in a handful of sentences.
- Lämmel & Schulte (TestCom 2006): controllable combinatorial coverage — $k$-context criteria between production coverage and full combinatorial explosion. Definitions are solid; the link to model quality was never the goal.
- Mairson (IPL, 1994): exact uniform sampling from $L(G)$ by size, via DP counts.
- Csmith (Yang et al., PLDI 2011): a grammar-plus-semantic-constraint C generator; **325+ bugs** reported in production compilers. This is the strongest evidence that grammar sampling reaches states hand-written suites miss — for *testing*, not for training.

**Established (neural synthesis on DSL grammars).**

- DeepCoder (Balog et al., ICLR 2017) and RobustFill (Devlin et al., ICML 2017): train on millions of uniformly sampled DSL programs; RobustFill reaches ~92% on the FlashFill benchmark suite at large beam.
- Shin et al. (ICLR 2019), *Synthetic Datasets for Neural Program Synthesis*: the first direct attack on this problem. Shows that uniform sampling over Karel and FlashFill grammars produces corpora whose program *and input* distributions are badly skewed, and that distribution-aware samplers improve held-out generalisation. Established at DSL scale; not replicated at language scale.
- BUSTLE (Odena et al., ICLR 2021) and DreamCoder (Ellis et al., PLDI 2021): learned search/library priors reduce the effective grammar the model must cover.

**Claimed but unablated.** Every LLM code-data pipeline — Textbooks Are All You Need (Gunasekar et al., 2023), Magicoder/OSS-Instruct (Wei et al., ICML 2024) — reports HumanEval/MBPP deltas only. None reports a coverage statistic of the generated corpus, none ablates coverage against yield. These results exist **only as benchmark numbers**; they do not license any claim about grammar coverage.

**Theory SOTA.** Nothing specific to this problem. The nearest results are negative learnability results (§4) about grammars themselves, not about training on them.

## 4. What Is Known

- **Syntactic coverage saturates almost immediately.** For any $G$, $C_1 = 1$ after $O(|R|)$ programs (Purdom 1972). At DSL scale ($|R| \approx 20$–$40$) this is dozens of programs against corpora of $10^6$. The metric carries no signal past the first minute of generation.
- **Semantic space grows far slower than syntactic space.** Bottom-up synthesisers prune by observational equivalence and get order-of-magnitude reductions in the enumerated frontier — the technique dates to TRANSIT (Udupa et al., PLDI 2013) and is standard in SyGuS solvers. The collapse ratio is measurable and large (see §10: 1645:1 on a 6-production grammar at size 9).
- **Growth constants are exact and cheap.** For the arithmetic grammar of §10, $t_n$ satisfies $T = 4z + 2zT^2$, giving $\alpha = \sqrt{32} \approx 5.66$ per node. Any grammar's $\alpha$ is computable in seconds.
- **Distribution shift dominates volume.** Shin et al. (2019) show models trained on uniform DSL samples degrade sharply on realistic held-out program distributions for Karel and FlashFill, at corpus sizes of $10^5$–$10^6$.
- **Repetition has a known cliff.** Muennighoff et al. (NeurIPS 2023) find ~4 epochs of repeated data are nearly as valuable as fresh data, with returns near zero past ~16, measured up to 9B parameters / 900B tokens. Semantically duplicate synthetic programs are *de facto* repetition, so grammar redundancy converts directly into wasted budget.
- **Grammar learnability is hard in the reverse direction.** Superfinite classes are not identifiable in the limit from positive data (Gold, 1967); DFA learning is hard under cryptographic assumptions (Kearns & Valiant, JACM 1994). Relevant because "infer the grammar of real code, then sample it" is not a shortcut.

## 5. What Is Not Known

- **Methodologically blocked.** No validated coverage statistic. $C_1$ saturates, $C_k$ has no principled $k$ and no normalisation across grammars, $C_{\mathrm{sem}}$ depends on an arbitrary input battery $I$ and has no denominator once $\mathcal{T}_{\le n}$ is uncountable in practice. The field measures corpus *size* and calls it coverage. This is the primary blocker.
- **Empirically open.** Whether the coverage–performance curve $\pi(C)$ saturates at some $C^\star < 1$, and where. Runnable today at 1B parameters on a mid-size DSL for well under $10^4$ GPU-hours. Nobody has published the curve.
- **Empirically open.** Whether $C_{\mathrm{sem}}$-maximising samplers beat uniform-by-size at matched token budget, for grammars with $|R| > 100$.
- **Theoretically open.** Any lower bound on the grammar gap $\pi^\star(G,B) - \pi_{\mathcal{D}^*}$ as a function of grammar statistics. No proof exists in either direction that a fixed $G$ imposes a hard ceiling.

## 6. Why It Is Hard

**The evaluation does not measure the thing it names, and the ground truth is absent.**

- *Coverage is defined syntactically but matters semantically.* A corpus at $C_1 = 1.0$ can denote three distinct functions. The names "100% grammar coverage" and "the model has seen the language" are not the same claim, and only the first is measurable cheaply.
- *No ground-truth target distribution.* $\mathcal{D}^*$ — the distribution of programs users will actually request — is unobserved. HumanEval (164 problems) and MBPP are proxies small enough that a coverage ablation's effect size sits inside their binomial noise: at 164 items, a 3-point pass@1 difference has a standard error of about 3.5 points.
- *Non-identifiability.* Sampler entropy, corpus size, dedup aggressiveness, and semantic diversity co-vary in every published pipeline. A gain attributed to "better coverage" is equally explained by fewer duplicates, i.e. more effective unique tokens.
- *Compute.* The decisive experiment is a 2-D sweep over (coverage level $\times$ token budget) with several seeds. At 1B parameters that is $\sim$30 pretraining runs; at 7B it is out of reach of most academic groups.

## 7. Current Research (as of 2026)

- **Semantic-diversity samplers.** Rejection sampling against an observational-equivalence hash, borrowed from SyGuS bottom-up enumeration, applied to training-corpus generation. Groups working on neurosymbolic synthesis (MIT/Ellis, UT Austin/Dillig-adjacent, Google DeepMind's BUSTLE lineage) are the natural home. *(frontier — verify.)*
- **Verifier-filtered self-generated code data.** Execution-filtered and unit-test-filtered synthetic corpora at industrial scale. Coverage is implicit and unreported. *(frontier — verify.)*
- **Formal-language probes of transformers.** Allen-Zhu & Li's CFG work shows transformers implement DP-like parsing on synthetic grammars; a plausible bridge to predicting which productions a model has internalised. *(frontier — verify.)*
- **Grammar-constrained decoding** at inference is now standard, which shifts the burden: the model no longer needs to learn syntax, only semantics — sharpening the case that syntactic coverage is the wrong axis. Established practice; the training-data implication is unstudied.

## 8. Concrete Next Experiment

**Question:** does downstream solve rate track semantic coverage $C_{\mathrm{sem}}$ or corpus size?

- **Grammar.** A SyGuS-style linear-integer-arithmetic + conditionals DSL, $|R| \approx 30$, plus a 40-production string DSL as replication.
- **Corpora.** Five arms, each **exactly 2B training tokens** of programs paired with 5 I/O examples:
  1. uniform-by-size (control arm);
  2. exact-dedup only;
  3. observational-equivalence dedup on a fixed battery $I$, $|I| = 20$;
  4. $C_2$-context-balanced sampling (equalise 2-context frequencies);
  5. semantic-coverage-greedy: accept a program only if its output hash is new.
- **Scale.** 1.4B-parameter decoder, trained from scratch, 3 seeds per arm = 15 runs, ~2,000 A100-hours total.
- **Held-out.** 500 target programs drawn from a *different* generator (human-written DSL solutions where available; otherwise a hand-specified idiom distribution), disjoint by semantic hash from all training corpora.
- **The deciding number.** $\Delta = \pi_{\text{arm 5}} - \pi_{\text{arm 1}}$, pass@10 on the 500 held-out tasks. With 500 items and 3 seeds, the standard error is $\approx 2.2$ points.
  - $\Delta \ge 8$ points $\Rightarrow$ semantic coverage is the controlling variable; corpus size at fixed coverage is waste, and $C_{\mathrm{sem}}$ becomes the reportable statistic.
  - $|\Delta| \le 3$ points $\Rightarrow$ coverage is not the axis; the field should stop invoking it and report effective unique tokens instead.
- **Secondary readout.** Plot $\pi$ against $C_{\mathrm{sem}}$ across all 15 runs; the knee location, if any, is the empirical $C^\star$.

## 10. Worked Example

Take the smallest realistic arithmetic DSL:

$$S \to x \mid y \mid 0 \mid 1 \mid S{+}S \mid S{-}S \qquad (|R| = 6)$$

**Syntactic count.** With $z$ marking nodes, $T(z) = 4z + 2z\,T(z)^2$. Expanding:

| size $n$ | 1 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| trees $t_n$ | 4 | 32 | 512 | 10,240 | 229,376 |

Total for $n \le 9$: **240,164** distinct derivation trees. Growth constant $\alpha = \sqrt{32} \approx 5.66$ per node.

**Production coverage.** Two sentences — `x + y` and `0 - 1` — fire all six productions. So $C_1 = 1.0$ at $|D| = 2$, against a syntactic space of 240,164. The metric is exhausted after two examples.

**Semantic count.** Every term denotes a linear form $ax + by + c$ with $a,b,c \in \mathbb{Z}$. A size-9 tree has at most 5 leaves; each leaf contributes $\pm 1$ to exactly one coefficient, so $|a|+|b|+|c| \le 5$ with matching parity, i.e. $|a|+|b|+|c| \in \{1,3,5\}$. Lattice points at $L_1$ radius $r$ in $\mathbb{Z}^3$ number $4r^2+2$:

$$6 + 38 + 102 = 146$$

**At most 146 distinct semantics.**

**The obstruction, in one ratio.**

```
syntactic trees (n<=9):   240,164
distinct semantics:        <=146
collapse ratio:            1,645 : 1
C_1 = 1.0 reached at:      2 programs
semantics covered by
  those 2 programs:        2/146  = 1.4%
```

A generator that samples uniformly to $n \le 9$ and reports "100% grammar coverage on 240k programs" has produced a corpus that is 1,645× redundant and whose headline statistic was already maxed out at the second sample. Under Muennighoff et al.'s repetition cliff, the 240k programs are worth roughly 146 unique items repeated 1,645 times — far past the ~16-epoch point where returns vanish. Scaling $n$ makes this worse, not better: syntax grows as $5.66^n$ while linear-form semantics grow polynomially, as $O(n^3)$.

That gap between an exponential numerator and a polynomial denominator is the problem. No published pipeline reports the denominator.
