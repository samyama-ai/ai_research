# DPO versus PPO Equivalence at Scale

> **Topic:** Alignment & Preference Learning · **ID:** `04-alignment/dpo-vs-ppo-equivalence-scale` · **Status:** empirically-open

## 1. Problem Statement

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is derived as the closed-form solution of the same KL-regularized reward-maximization objective that PPO-based RLHF optimizes by sampling. The derivation is exact **at the optimum, over the full support of the response space, with unlimited data**. The open problem is whether the two procedures land in the same place under the finite conditions people actually run them.

Three variants, with different difficulty:

- **Theory variant.** Under what conditions on the preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$, the policy class $\Pi$, and the optimizer do the DPO and PPO solutions coincide (or provably diverge)? The optimum-equivalence result is proved; the *finite-sample, finite-class, finite-compute* statement is not.
- **Method variant.** Given equal compute, equal preference data, and equally tuned hyperparameters, does one method dominate on held-out human preference at model scale $\geq 70$B? Reported gaps exist but confound tuning effort, data regime (on-policy vs. off-policy), and reward-model access.
- **Measurement variant.** What is the right *distance* between two aligned policies? Win-rate against a judge is a scalar that saturates; KL divergence to the reference is not comparable across methods with different implicit $\beta$. There is no agreed metric for "these two alignment procedures produced the same policy."

Solving it means: a compute-matched, data-matched protocol at frontier scale plus a stated decision number, with a theoretical account of the residual gap in terms of coverage of $\mathcal{D}$.

## 2. Formal Setting

Prompts $x \sim \rho$, responses $y$ from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (the SFT checkpoint). Preference pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$ are drawn from an annotation process; the Bradley–Terry (BT) model assumes
$$p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r^\star(x,y_w) - r^\star(x,y_l)\big).$$

The shared objective:
$$J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,\, y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big)\big],$$
with the analytic maximizer $\pi^\star(y|x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$. Inverting gives the DPO loss
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right)\right].$$

PPO instead fits $\hat r_\phi$ on the same $\mathcal{D}$ and maximizes $J$ by clipped policy-gradient steps on samples from $\pi_\theta$.

Quantities **as measured**:

- **Compute** $C$: total training FLOPs, including reward-model training and generation for PPO. Generation dominates PPO cost; a fair match reports $C$ per arm, not epochs.
- **KL budget**: $\widehat{\mathrm{KL}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x_i)}$ on $y_i \sim \pi_\theta$ (the $k_1$ estimator; $k_3$ is lower-variance). Sequence-level, in nats, reported at fixed decoding temperature.
- **Alignment quality**: win rate $w$ against a fixed baseline under a fixed judge, with $n \geq 1000$ prompts, position-swapped, 95% CI $\approx \pm 3$ points at $n=1000$.
- **Policy distance**: $\mathbb{E}_{x}\,\mathrm{JSD}(\pi_{\text{DPO}} \| \pi_{\text{PPO}})$ estimated by sampling — the only method-symmetric distance available.

Assumptions and their status in practice:

| Assumption | Status |
|---|---|
| BT model of annotators | **Violated** — intransitive and heterogeneous raters (Munos et al. 2023 motivates the Nash formulation). |
| $\mathcal{D}$ covers $\pi_\theta$'s support | **Violated** — DPO is trained on off-policy pairs; PPO samples on-policy. This is the main suspected source of the gap. |
| $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the sampling policy for $\mathcal{D}$ | **Usually violated** — preference sets are often generated by other models. |
| Unconstrained policy class | Violated by construction; the analytic $\pi^\star$ is generally unrepresentable. |
| Exact optimization | Violated — both arms are early-stopped well before convergence. |

## 3. State of the Art

**Theory SOTA (established).** Rafailov et al. (NeurIPS 2023) prove the DPO/RLHF objective equivalence at the optimum with unconstrained $\pi$. Rafailov et al. (COLM 2024, *From $r$ to $Q^\star$*) extend it: DPO's implicit reward is a valid $Q$-function in the token-level MDP, so the equivalence is not merely a bandit artifact. Azar et al. (AISTATS 2024) show the reverse: the equivalence relies on the BT link, and with deterministic preferences ($p \to 1$) the DPO objective's regularization degenerates — the KL term stops binding — motivating $\Psi$PO/IPO. These are theorems, not benchmark numbers.

**Empirical SOTA (claimed, partially ablated).** Xu et al. (ICML 2024) report PPO beating DPO across dialogue and code, with their PPO-trained 34B model exceeding AlphaCode-41B on CodeContests — a benchmark number, not a controlled equivalence test, and compute is not matched. Ivison et al. (NeurIPS 2024, Tulu 2.5) run the closest thing to a controlled comparison: same preference data, same SFT init, 13B and 70B, reporting PPO ahead of DPO by roughly 1–2 points on average and up to ~7–8 points on GSM8K-style reasoning. That study *is* ablated on data source and reward-model size but not compute-matched.

**Contrary evidence.** Llama 3 (Grattafiori et al. 2024) used iterative DPO rather than PPO at 405B and reported it as the better cost/quality point — a deployment decision, unablated against a PPO arm at that scale. No public frontier-scale head-to-head exists.

## 4. What Is Known

- **Optimum equivalence** holds exactly, unconstrained policy class, BT preferences (Rafailov et al. 2023).
- **On-policy data matters more than the loss family.** Tang et al. (ICML 2024) show a persistent online-vs-offline gap that survives matching the loss, dataset size, and reward model, at up to ~7B scale — the gap tracks *data freshness*, not DPO-vs-PPO per se. Tajwar et al. (ICML 2024) reach the same conclusion: on-policy sampling plus negative gradient dominates, across method families.
- **Iterated DPO closes much of the gap.** Dong et al. (2024, RLHF Workflow) obtain PPO-competitive results from online iterative DPO at 8B.
- **PPO's advantage is partly implementation.** Huang et al. (2024) reproduce OpenAI's RLHF only after fixing many undocumented details; Ahmadian et al. (ACL 2024) show REINFORCE-style baselines match or beat PPO on standard RLHF, i.e. PPO's machinery is not the source of its wins.
- **Both arms overoptimize their reward proxy.** Gao et al. (ICML 2023) give the $\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$-shaped scaling law for reward-model overoptimization: proxy reward keeps rising as gold reward turns over, at KL budgets of tens of nats, measured at up to 3B policies with a 6B gold RM.
- Scale of all controlled evidence: **$\leq$ 70B**, mostly $\leq$ 13B. Nothing at $\geq 300$B.

## 5. What Is Not Known

- **Theoretically open.** No finite-sample bound comparing DPO's and PPO's suboptimality gaps as a function of dataset coverage $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D}, \pi^\star)$ and policy-class capacity. No proof of whether the gap shrinks, is constant, or grows with model scale $N$.
- **Empirically open.** The compute-matched, data-matched, both-arms-equally-tuned comparison at $\geq 70$B (ideally $\geq 300$B) is runnable today by five or six labs and has not been published. This is the load-bearing gap.
- **Methodologically blocked.** "Same policy" has no accepted metric. Win rate saturates and is judge-dependent; KL-to-reference is not comparable across arms with different effective $\beta$; sampled JSD between two frontier policies has no established estimator variance at practical sample sizes.

## 6. Why It Is Hard

The specific obstruction is **compute-matching a confounded comparison**. PPO spends most of its FLOPs on generation; DPO spends none. Any experiment must choose whether to equalize FLOPs, wall-clock, or preference-label count — and the three orderings can differ. Worse, the honest comparison requires equal *tuning* effort ($\beta$, learning rate, KL coefficient, RM size, sampling temperature), and tuning at 70B costs more than the run itself, so published comparisons are systematically biased toward whichever arm the authors know better. Layered on top: the evaluation (LLM-judge win rate) does not measure the named quantity — it measures agreement with a judge that has known length and style biases, so a 2-point gap is not distinguishable from a verbosity difference without length-controlled decoding.

## 7. Current Research (as of 2026)

- **Online/iterative direct methods** — closing the loop by regenerating preferences from the current policy (Dong et al.; Meta's Llama line; AI2's Tulu line). Directionally, the field has converged on "the online/offline axis is the real one" *(frontier — verify for 2026 results)*.
- **Game-theoretic reformulations** that drop BT: Nash learning from human feedback (Munos et al., DeepMind).
- **Reference-free simplifications** (SimPO, Meng et al. 2024) that break the DPO derivation deliberately and still perform well — evidence the derivation is not what makes DPO work.
- **Token-level credit assignment** connecting DPO's implicit $Q$-function to process supervision (Rafailov et al.).
- Open, unclaimed: a public compute-matched frontier-scale arm. Cost, not interest, is the barrier.

## 8. Concrete Next Experiment

**Scale.** One base model at 70B, SFT checkpoint fixed and shared. One fixed preference set: 60k pairs, single annotation source.

**Arms (three, FLOP-matched to $\pm 5\%$, total budget $C$ per arm, including RM training and generation for the PPO arm):**
1. Offline DPO on the fixed 60k.
2. PPO with a 7B reward model trained on the same 60k.
3. **Control arm:** iterative online DPO — 3 rounds, regenerating pairs from the current policy, labeled by the *same* 7B RM the PPO arm uses. This is the arm that isolates "online data" from "policy-gradient loss."

Tune each arm with an equal, pre-registered budget of 8 hyperparameter trials at 7B, transferred by fixed rule.

**Decision number.** Evaluate all arms on a common KL–reward frontier: sweep the regularization strength per arm, plot gold win rate (held-out human preference, $n=2000$, length-controlled decoding, position-swapped) against sequence-level $\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}$ from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and report

$$\Delta = \max_{\widehat{\mathrm{KL}} \leq 20\text{ nats}} w_{\text{PPO}} - \max_{\widehat{\mathrm{KL}} \leq 20\text{ nats}} w_{\text{online-DPO}}.$$

If $|\Delta| < 2$ points (inside the $\pm 2.2$-point 95% CI at $n=2000$), the method variant is settled: the loss family does not matter, only data freshness does. If $\Delta > 4$ points, PPO's advantage is real and independent of on-policy data, and the theory variant becomes the live question.

## 10. Worked Example

Take a 13B SFT policy, 60k preference pairs, and hold the reward model fixed at 7B. Suppose the measured frontier is:

| Arm | $\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}$ (nats) | Proxy RM score | Gold win rate ($n=1000$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Offline DPO, $\beta=0.1$ | 31 | 0.82 | 58.1% |
| Offline DPO, $\beta=0.5$ | 9 | 0.44 | 55.4% |
| PPO, KL coef 0.05 | 28 | 0.91 | 60.3% |
| Online iterative DPO | 26 | 0.88 | 60.0% |

The naive read — "PPO beats DPO by 2.2 points" — collapses twice.

First, the comparison is off-frontier. DPO at $\beta=0.1$ sits at 31 nats and PPO at 28; Gao et al.'s law says gold reward falls off roughly as a $\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$-shaped hump, so the two arms are being read at different points of an inverted-U, and the 2.2 points partly measure "DPO overshot the KL budget," not "DPO's loss is worse."

Second, the CI. At $n=1000$ with $w \approx 0.60$, the standard error is $\sqrt{0.6\cdot0.4/1000} \approx 1.55\%$, so the 95% CI is $\pm 3.0$ points. A 2.2-point gap is **not significant**. And PPO vs. online DPO differs by 0.3 points — noise.

That is the obstruction made concrete: the gap the literature argues about is the same order as the KL-placement error and the judge's sampling error combined, and the arm that removes the confound (online DPO) sits inside the noise band of the arm it is supposed to lose to. Deciding the question needs $n \geq 2000$, a swept frontier rather than single points, and length-controlled decoding — at a scale where each swept point costs a 70B training run.
