# Intrinsic Dimension Scaling with Model Size

> **Topic:** Parameter-Efficient Adaptation · **ID:** `13-parameter-efficient-adaptation/intrinsic-dimension-scaling-model-size` · **Status:** empirically-open

## 1. Problem Statement

**Input.** A family of pretrained models $\{f_{\theta^{(N)}}\}$ indexed by parameter count $N$, trained on the same corpus with the same recipe; a downstream task $T$ with train/test splits; a target performance level.

**Output.** The intrinsic dimension $d_{90}(N, T)$ — the smallest dimension of a random affine subspace of parameter space in which optimization reaches 90% of full fine-tuning performance — and its scaling law in $N$.

**Decision predicate.** Fit $d_{90}(N) \propto N^{-\alpha}$. Is $\alpha > 0$, and does it hold across model families, tasks, and pretraining-data budgets?

Three variants, with different difficulty:

- **Measurement.** Is $d_{90}$ even well defined as a comparable quantity across $N$? The 90% threshold is relative to a baseline that itself moves with $N$, so the "same" measurement is a different task at each scale.
- **Method.** Given a target adapter budget, can $d_{90}(N)$ predict the rank/parameter count a PEFT method needs — before running the fine-tune?
- **Theory.** Does a transversality or NTK argument predict the observed decrease, or does theory predict $d_{90}$ should be roughly *constant* in $N$?

Solving it means: a predictive law, validated out-of-sample at a scale not used to fit it, with the confounds in §6 controlled.

## 2. Formal Setting

Let $\theta^{(N)}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ be the pretrained weights, $D \approx N$. Subspace fine-tuning reparameterizes

$$\theta = \theta_0 + P\,\theta_d, \qquad P \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d}, \quad \theta_d \in \mathbb{R}^{d},$$

with $P$ a fixed random projection with (approximately) orthonormal columns, generated by a Fastfood transform (Le, Sarlós, Smola, ICML 2013) so that the matrix–vector product costs $O(D \log D)$ time and $O(D)$ memory rather than $O(Dd)$. Only $\theta_d$ is trained; $\theta_0$ and $P$ are frozen.

**Measured quantities.**

- $A_{\mathrm{full}}(N)$: task metric after full fine-tuning of all $D$ parameters, best over a fixed hyperparameter grid, on a held-out split.
- $A_d(N)$: same metric after training $\theta_d$ only, best over the same grid size.
- $$d_{90}(N) \;=\; \min\{\, d : A_d(N) \ge 0.9\, A_{\mathrm{full}}(N) \,\},$$ located by binary search over $d \in \{2^k\}$ then refined. Every $d$ requires a full fine-tuning run, so one $d_{90}$ costs $\sim 10$–$20$ runs.
- Structure-aware variant (SAID): a per-layer scale $w \in \mathbb{R}^{L}$ is trained jointly, $\theta = \theta_0 + w \odot_{\text{layer}} P\theta_{d-L}$.

**Assumptions, and which are violated.**

1. *$P$ is isotropic and task-independent.* Holds by construction. But it means $d_{90}$ measures reachability of the solution set by a **generic** subspace, not the dimension of the task-specific subspace — Zhang et al. (ACL 2023) show task subspaces found by non-random methods are far smaller.
2. *The 90% threshold is comparable across $N$.* **Violated.** $A_{\mathrm{full}}$ rises with $N$ (e.g. MRPC accuracy $\approx 88\% \to 91\%$ from base to large), so the absolute bar moves. Worse, on saturated GLUE tasks $0.9 A_{\mathrm{full}}$ is beatable by a linear probe or even a majority-class-plus-heuristic baseline, making $d_{90}$ measure the wrong thing.
3. *$d_{90}$ is a property of the loss landscape, not the optimizer.* **Violated.** $d_{90}$ is an optimization-reachability quantity; it depends on learning rate, schedule, and steps. Li et al. (2018) report sensitivity to these.
4. *Solution sets are manifolds of well-defined codimension.* Assumed by the transversality intuition; unverified for transformer losses with non-smooth components (LayerNorm, ReLU/GELU kinks, discrete argmax metrics).

## 3. State of the Art

**Empirical SOTA — established.** Li, Farkhoor, Liu, Yosinski, *Measuring the Intrinsic Dimension of Objective Landscapes* (ICLR 2018) introduced the random-subspace measurement and the Fastfood implementation. Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer, Gupta, *Intrinsic Dimensionality Explains the Effectiveness of Language Model Fine-Tuning* (ACL 2021) applied it to pretrained LMs and reported two regularities: $d_{90}$ falls as pretraining proceeds, and $d_{90}$ is smaller for larger models in the same family. This is the source of the "larger models are more compressible to adapt" claim.

**Claimed but unablated.** The specific inverse-scaling reading — that $d_{90}$ shrinks *monotonically in $N$* as a law — rests on a two-point comparison per task (RoBERTa-base vs RoBERTa-large, 125M vs 355M) plus a BART-large point. Two points do not fit an exponent. No ablation separates model size from the confounds in §6 (different pretraining token counts, different $A_{\mathrm{full}}$ baselines, different optimal learning rates).

**Benchmark-number-only.** LoRA (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) reports $r = 1$ or $2$ sufficient on GPT-3 175B for WikiSQL and MNLI. This is quoted as evidence for intrinsic-dimension scaling. It is not the same quantity: rank-1 LoRA on all attention projections of GPT-3 is still $\sim 4.7$M trainable parameters, three orders of magnitude above the $d_{90}$ values reported for RoBERTa. Rank and subspace dimension are different budgets.

**Theory SOTA.** No scaling theorem. The available argument is transversality: if the $90\%$-solution set is a manifold of codimension $c$ in $\mathbb{R}^D$, a random affine $d$-subspace intersects it with probability $1$ iff $d \ge c$ (Li et al., 2018, informal). This predicts $d_{90} \approx c$, *independent of $D$*. So the observed decrease requires $c$ itself to shrink with $N$ — which nothing proves.

## 4. What Is Known

- **Non-pretrained baselines (Li et al., ICLR 2018).** $d_{90} \approx 750$ for a 784–200–200–10 MLP on MNIST ($D \approx 200$k); $d_{90} \approx 290$ for LeNet on MNIST; $d_{90} \approx 2900$ for a small CNN on CIFAR-10. Across MNIST architectures spanning roughly an order of magnitude in $D$, $d_{90}$ varied by a factor of $\sim 3$ and not monotonically in $D$ — the earliest evidence against a clean $D$-dependence.
- **Pretrained LMs (Aghajanyan et al., ACL 2021).** On MRPC (3.7k examples), RoBERTa-large ($355$M) reaches $d_{90} \approx 207$ against RoBERTa-base ($125$M) at $\approx 896$ — a $4.3\times$ drop for a $2.8\times$ size increase. Order-of-magnitude values are $10^2$–$10^3$ for GLUE-scale tasks, i.e. $d_{90}/D \sim 10^{-6}$.
- **Pretraining, not size, moves $d_{90}$ strongly.** In the same paper, $d_{90}$ for a fixed RoBERTa architecture falls by roughly an order of magnitude over pretraining checkpoints. Model size and pretraining amount are therefore both live explanations, and in the reported comparisons they are confounded.
- **Low rank suffices at 175B for some tasks.** LoRA $r{=}1$ matches full fine-tuning on WikiSQL/MNLI with GPT-3 175B (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) — established as a benchmark result, replicated widely.
- **Low rank is not equivalent to full fine-tuning.** Biderman et al., *LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less* (TMLR 2024): on code and math continued-pretraining at 7B/13B, LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning by large margins at ranks up to 256. Shuttleworth et al. (2024) report "intruder dimensions" — singular directions in LoRA updates absent from full fine-tuning — so equal task accuracy does not imply the same solution.
- **Data intrinsic dimension is a separate, smaller quantity.** Pope et al. (ICLR 2021) estimate ID $\approx 26$–$43$ for ImageNet with the MLE/TwoNN estimators. Not the same object; frequently conflated in secondary literature.

## 5. What Is Not Known

- **Empirically open (the main gap).** No one has measured $d_{90}$ across $\ge 5$ model sizes in a family with *controlled pretraining data* (Pythia, OLMo). The experiment is runnable today; it has not been run. The exponent $\alpha$ has no published estimate with error bars, and the largest published $d_{90}$ measurement remains at $\sim 355$M parameters — two to three orders of magnitude below frontier scale.
- **Empirically open.** Whether $d_{90}$ depends on $N$ at fixed pretraining tokens, or only on tokens at fixed $N$. Aghajanyan et al. vary both.
- **Theoretically open.** No proof that the codimension $c$ of the $\epsilon$-solution set decreases with width or depth. NTK-regime analysis gives the linearized case; the fine-tuning regime is not obviously linear at small $d$.
- **Methodologically blocked.** The relative-90% threshold is not a scale-invariant measurement (§2, assumption 2). Until a threshold is defined that is comparable across $N$ — absolute metric target, loss-based target, or normalization against a linear probe — reported cross-scale $d_{90}$ ratios are not interpretable as a scaling law.
- **Open.** The mapping $d_{90} \mapsto$ LoRA rank. There is no derivation, and the two budgets differ by $10^3$–$10^4$ in practice.

## 6. Why It Is Hard

**Confounded measurement is the primary obstruction.** $d_{90}$ is defined relative to $A_{\mathrm{full}}(N)$, which changes with $N$. When $A_{\mathrm{full}}$ rises, $0.9A_{\mathrm{full}}$ is a *harder* absolute bar; when the task saturates, it becomes a *trivially easy* one. Both effects move $d_{90}$ with $N$ with no change in the landscape. On MRPC the majority-class baseline is $68.4\%$ accuracy, and $0.9 \times 91\% = 81.9\%$ — so the measured $d_{90}$ describes the dimension needed to clear a bar only $13.5$ points above majority class. That gap shrinks as models improve.

**Second obstruction: cost.** One $d_{90}$ point is $10$–$20$ fine-tuning runs. Five model sizes $\times$ four tasks $\times$ three seeds $\times$ 15 runs $= 900$ fine-tunes. At $\ge 7$B the Fastfood projection must be applied every step across all $D$ parameters, adding $O(D\log D)$ work per step and forcing the full parameter tensor to stay resident — subspace training at 7B costs *more* memory per step than LoRA, not less.

**Third: non-identifiability of the optimizer contribution.** A failure at dimension $d$ may mean the subspace misses the solution set, or that the hyperparameter grid missed the working learning rate. These are not distinguishable without an exhaustive sweep, and the required learning rate scale changes with $d$ and $N$.

## 7. Current Research (as of 2026)

- **Rank-allocation methods** — AdaLoRA (Zhang et al., ICLR 2023) allocates rank per layer by SVD importance; treated as an implicit per-layer intrinsic-dimension estimate, though not calibrated against $d_{90}$.
- **Task-subspace estimation** — Zhang et al. (ACL 2023) find task-specific subspaces of dimension $\sim 5$–$100$ by decomposing many fine-tuned checkpoints, far below random-subspace $d_{90}$. The gap between generic and task-adapted subspace dimension is an active measurement question.
- **Controlled model suites** — Pythia (Biderman et al., ICML 2023) and OLMo make the token-controlled sweep possible for the first time; *(frontier — verify)* whether any group has published $d_{90}$ across the Pythia ladder.
- **LoRA-vs-full-FT equivalence** — Databricks/Columbia lines (Biderman et al. 2024; Shuttleworth et al. 2024) argue rank sufficiency is task-dependent and that instruction-tuning-like tasks are the easy case.
- **Data-side ID estimators** — TwoNN (Facco et al., *Scientific Reports* 2017) and Ansuini et al. (NeurIPS 2019) on representation ID; occasionally proposed as a cheap proxy for parameter-space $d_{90}$, with no established link.

## 8. Concrete Next Experiment

**Scale.** Pythia at $\{160\text{M}, 410\text{M}, 1.0\text{B}, 1.4\text{B}, 2.8\text{B}, 6.9\text{B}\}$ — six sizes, identical data in identical order, all at 300B tokens. Tasks: MNLI (392k), BoolQ, and a held-out code-completion task (non-saturated, so the threshold is meaningful). Fastfood projection, $d \in \{2^4, \dots, 2^{16}\}$, binary search, 3 seeds.

**Control arms.** (i) *Absolute-threshold arm:* fix the target metric to a constant $A^\star$ across all $N$ (set at the 160M model's full-FT score), removing the moving-baseline confound. (ii) *Token-controlled arm:* the 1.4B model at 4 pretraining checkpoints (20B/60B/150B/300B tokens) to separate size from pretraining amount. (iii) *Random-init arm:* the same sizes with untrained weights, to show the effect is pretraining-dependent.

**Deciding number.** Fit $\log d_{90} = \beta - \alpha \log N$ over the six sizes in the absolute-threshold arm. The question is settled by the bootstrap 95% CI on $\alpha$: if it excludes $0$ and the value agrees within CI with the token-controlled arm's residual, the scaling law is real; if the CI covers $0$ while the token-controlled arm shows a clear trend, the effect is pretraining, not size. Prior from the two published points implies $\alpha \approx \log(4.3)/\log(2.8) \approx 1.4$; anything below $0.3$ falsifies the strong reading.

**Budget.** $\sim 6 \times 3 \times 3 \times 15 \approx 800$ fine-tunes, dominated by the 6.9B arm; roughly $10^4$ A100-hours. Large but not frontier-scale — this is why the problem is empirically open rather than blocked.

## 10. Worked Example

Take the MRPC pair from Aghajanyan et al. and carry the confound through.

| | RoBERTa-base | RoBERTa-large |
|---|---|---|
| $D$ | $1.25 \times 10^{8}$ | $3.55 \times 10^{8}$ |
| $A_{\mathrm{full}}$ (acc.) | $\approx 88\%$ | $\approx 91\%$ |
| $0.9 A_{\mathrm{full}}$ | $79.2\%$ | $81.9\%$ |
| $d_{90}$ | $\approx 896$ | $\approx 207$ |
| $d_{90}/D$ | $7.2 \times 10^{-6}$ | $5.8 \times 10^{-7}$ |

Naive exponent: $\alpha = \log(896/207)/\log(355/125) = 1.466/1.044 = 1.40$.

Now the obstruction. MRPC's majority class is $68.4\%$. The base model's bar sits $10.8$ points above majority; the large model's sits $13.5$ points above. These are not the same task. Recompute both against a **fixed absolute** bar of $79.2\%$ — the base model's own threshold. RoBERTa-large clears $79.2\%$ at some $d' \le 207$, and because the target is now $2.7$ points easier for it, $d' < 207$ strictly. The measured ratio therefore *overstates*, and the direction of the bias is not recoverable without rerunning the large model's binary search at the lower bar. Nobody has published that number.

Extrapolate anyway, to see what is at stake. At $\alpha = 1.40$, a 70B model gives

$$d_{90}(70\text{B}) = 207 \times \left(\tfrac{7\times10^{10}}{3.55\times 10^{8}}\right)^{-1.40} \approx 207 \times 197^{-1.40} \approx 0.13,$$

i.e. under one dimension — nonsense. So either $\alpha$ is much smaller than the two-point estimate, or the law saturates, or $d_{90}$ hits a floor set by task information content ($\log_2$ of the label space times something, at minimum). Meanwhile the empirical anchor at 175B is LoRA $r{=}1$ on WikiSQL — which is $4.7 \times 10^{6}$ trainable parameters, seven orders of magnitude *above* the extrapolation. The two "evidence" points for the same claim disagree by $10^{7}$. That gap, not the sign of $\alpha$, is what the experiment in §8 has to close.
