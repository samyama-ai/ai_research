# Byte-Level Models Matching Subword Models at Scale

> **Topic:** Tokenization & Vocabulary · **ID:** `01-tokenization/byte-level-parity-at-scale` · **Status:** empirically-open

## 1. Problem Statement

A subword tokenizer (BPE, Unigram, WordPiece) is a fixed, corpus-fitted preprocessing stage that a language model cannot revise. It fixes the input alphabet, the compression rate, and — through merge statistics estimated on one corpus mixture — the per-language and per-domain cost of a character. Byte-level models delete this stage: input and output are raw UTF-8 bytes.

The problem: **does a byte-level architecture reach or beat a subword baseline at equal training compute and equal inference cost, at frontier scale?**

Three variants, with different difficulty:

- **Measurement.** Define a comparison that is fair across models with different sequence granularity. Perplexity is not comparable across vocabularies; bits-per-byte is, but FLOP-matching and latency-matching disagree about what "equal cost" means.
- **Method.** Build a byte model whose compute-matched bits-per-byte and downstream accuracy are $\ge$ a strong BPE baseline at $\ge 10^{23}$ FLOPs. Current byte architectures do this by re-introducing learned pooling — patches, chunks, or hierarchies — so the honest question is whether *learned, input-conditional* segmentation beats *fixed, frequency-fitted* segmentation.
- **Theory.** Explain when tokenization helps. No result predicts the compute-optimal granularity from properties of the data distribution.

Solving it means: a public, compute-matched run at $\ge$ 8B parameters where the byte model's aggregate downstream score is not worse and its per-token wall-clock is not worse, with the tokenizer baseline tuned as hard as the byte arm.

## 2. Formal Setting

Let $\mathcal{B} = \{0,\dots,255\}$ and let a document be $b_{1:N} \in \mathcal{B}^N$. A tokenizer is an injective encoder $\tau: \mathcal{B}^* \to \mathcal{V}^*$ with vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$, producing $x_{1:T} = \tau(b_{1:N})$.

**Compression rate**, measured directly on the eval corpus:
$$\rho(\tau) = \frac{N}{T} \quad \text{(bytes per token)}.$$
Typical: $\rho \approx 4.0$–$4.8$ for English with a 128k vocabulary, $\rho \approx 1.0$–$2.0$ for Telugu, Thai, or Amharic with the same vocabulary.

**The only cross-granularity comparable loss** is bits-per-byte, obtained by rescaling the model's mean token NLL:
$$\mathrm{BPB} = \frac{1}{N\ln 2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} -\ln p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \frac{\overline{\mathrm{NLL}}_{\text{tok}}}{\rho \ln 2}.$$
Measured as: total eval NLL in nats divided by total eval bytes, over identical raw byte strings — not identical token counts.

**Two cost budgets**, which must be reported separately:
$$C_{\text{train}} \approx 6\,P\,T_{\text{train}} \ \text{FLOPs}, \qquad \text{and} \quad L_{\text{gen}} = \text{wall-clock ms per generated byte at fixed batch and hardware}.$$
A byte model with patch size $p$ has a small local module at byte rate and a large global module at rate $1/p$; $6PT$ therefore requires per-module accounting, $P_{\text{global}}T/p + P_{\text{local}}T$, not a single $P$.

**The decision predicate**, at fixed $C_{\text{train}}$ and $L_{\text{gen}}$:
$$\Delta = \mathrm{BPB}_{\text{byte}} - \mathrm{BPB}_{\text{BPE}} \le 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \text{downstream score gap} \ge 0.$$

**Assumptions, and which fail.**
- *Equal-FLOP implies equal cost.* Violated. Byte models are memory-bandwidth-bound at short context and their entropy-based or dynamic chunkers add a serial dependency at decode; FLOP parity understates their latency.
- *BPB is tokenizer-independent.* Holds as an arithmetic identity, but only if both models are scored on byte-identical text with identical normalization (NFC, whitespace, BOM). Silent normalization inside a tokenizer breaks it.
- *The BPE baseline is optimal.* Violated in the other direction — most reported comparisons use one off-the-shelf vocabulary size, unswept.
- *Chinchilla-style scaling laws transfer across granularity.* Unverified. Data-optimal $T$ is quoted in tokens; the byte model's "token" is not the same object.

## 3. State of the Art

**Established.**
- **ByT5** (Xue et al., *TACL* 2022) — pure byte-level T5, parameter-matched to mT5. Competitive or better on short-input and noisy-text tasks (transliteration, TweetQA, spelling-corrupted inputs), worse on long-input tasks, and materially more expensive per unit of text. This is the clean negative result for *unpooled* byte models.
- **CANINE** (Clark et al., *TACL* 2022) — character input with downsampling, beats mBERT on TyDi QA with fewer parameters. Encoder-only; says nothing about generative scaling.
- **MegaByte** (Yu et al., *NeurIPS* 2023) — fixed patches of 8 bytes, global + local decoder, up to ~1.5B parameters, million-byte contexts. Establishes that patching removes the quadratic byte-length penalty.
- **MambaByte** (Wang et al., *COLM* 2024) — token-free SSM; a 353M-parameter model outperforms compute-matched MegaByte configurations on byte-level LM benchmarks.

**Claimed at scale, partially ablated.**
- **Byte Latent Transformer** (Pagnoni et al., Meta, 2024; *ACL* 2025) — entropy-based dynamic patching, trained to 8B parameters on 4T bytes. Reports training-FLOP-matched parity with a Llama-3 tokenizer baseline plus up to ~50% inference FLOP reduction at larger patch sizes. This is the strongest existing evidence, and it is the closest thing to a positive answer. Unablated: whether the BPE baseline's vocabulary and data mixture were swept as hard, and whether FLOP savings survive as wall-clock savings under a real serving stack.
- **H-Net** (Hwang, Wang, Gu, 2025) — end-to-end learned dynamic chunking, no external tokenizer or entropy model; reported to exceed a BPE Transformer of matched compute around the ~1B scale, with the largest gains on Chinese, code, and DNA. Benchmark numbers exist at ~1B; frontier-scale replication does not.

**Benchmark-number-only.** All published byte-vs-BPE "parity" claims above ~1B parameters come from a single lab's run each, on that lab's data mixture, with no independent reproduction.

## 4. What Is Known

- Pure byte models pay a sequence-length tax of roughly $\rho \approx 4$–$5\times$ in English. Reported at 300M–13B (ByT5 sizes: 300M–12.9B).
- Byte models are more robust to orthographic noise and better at character-level manipulation (spelling, transliteration, digit handling). Measured at ByT5 scale and reproduced informally many times.
- Tokenizer cost is grossly unequal across languages: Petrov et al. (*NeurIPS* 2023) measure up to ~15$\times$ more tokens for the same content in some languages than in English under a common tokenizer; Ahia et al. (*EMNLP* 2023) show the same disparity turns into a direct API cost and context-window penalty. Byte models make this disparity a property of the script's UTF-8 encoding (1–4 bytes/char) rather than of a fitted merge table.
- Patching/pooling recovers most of the compute gap: MegaByte, SpaceByte (Slagle, 2024), and Dynamic Token Pooling (Nawrot et al., *ACL* 2023) all show that byte-level input with learned or heuristic boundaries is compute-competitive at 100M–1.5B.
- At 8B / 4T bytes, BLT reports no BPB regression versus a tokenizer baseline. This is the largest scale at which anything like parity has been reported.

## 5. What Is Not Known

- **Empirically open (the core gap).** Whether byte-level parity holds at $\ge 10^{24}$ training FLOPs against a *tuned* subword baseline, and whether the byte curve's slope in $\log C$ is equal, steeper, or shallower. The experiment is runnable today; it costs frontier-scale compute and nobody has published it with both arms tuned.
- **Empirically open.** Whether patch-size scaling is safe: BLT's inference savings grow with patch size, but no published law relates optimal patch size $p^\star$ to $P$ and $T$.
- **Methodologically blocked.** "Equal inference cost" has no agreed definition across granularities. FLOP-matched, latency-matched, and KV-cache-bytes-matched comparisons rank the same pair of models differently, and no paper reports all three.
- **Theoretically open.** No proof that any fixed-vocabulary tokenizer is or is not lossy in the information-theoretic sense that matters for next-byte prediction. Related known result: tokenization interacts with the model's ability to represent the true byte-level process (Rajaraman et al., 2024, show tokenization can make simple Markov sources learnable by transformers that otherwise fail) — but this argues *for* tokenization, and its converse is unproven.

## 6. Why It Is Hard

The obstruction is **confounded measurement compounded by asymmetric baseline effort**, not compute alone.

Every published byte-vs-BPE comparison varies at least three things at once: granularity, architecture (byte models add a local encoder/decoder and a boundary predictor), and effective context in bytes. A win cannot be attributed to removing the tokenizer. The natural control — the same architecture with a tokenizer — is rarely run, because a hierarchical patching model with a BPE front-end is an odd artifact nobody wants to train.

Second, the baseline is asymmetrically tuned. Vocabulary size, pre-tokenization regex, digit and whitespace handling, and byte-fallback are all free parameters worth several percent of BPB; the byte arm is the paper's contribution and gets the sweeps.

Third, latency parity is not FLOP parity. Entropy-based patching requires running a small byte-level LM to decide boundaries at decode time — a serial step that does not appear in $6PT$.

## 7. Current Research (as of 2026)

- **Dynamic/learned chunking** — H-Net-style end-to-end boundary learning, replacing the separate entropy model. Carnegie Mellon (Gu), and follow-ups on multi-stage hierarchies. *(frontier — verify)*
- **Entropy-patched production models** — Meta's BLT line; the open question in the group is scaling patch size with model size. *(frontier — verify)*
- **Scaling laws across granularity** — attempts to fit $\mathrm{BPB}(C)$ curves separately for byte and subword families to find a crossover compute $C^\star$. No published crossover estimate. *(frontier — verify)*
- **Multilingual equity as the primary motivation** — byte models framed as the fix for the token-cost disparity documented by Petrov et al. and Ahia et al.
- **Adjacent**: tokenizer transfer/retrofitting, which competes with byte models by making the subword stage cheap to replace rather than deleting it.

## 8. Concrete Next Experiment

**The 3B, three-arm, latency-matched ablation.**

- **Scale.** $P \approx 3$B non-embedding parameters, $\approx 6\times10^{22}$ FLOPs (≈ 300B subword tokens ≈ 1.3T bytes), one data mixture, one seed per arm, plus one repeat seed on the two closest arms.
- **Arms.**
  1. *Control A (tuned BPE):* standard decoder, vocabulary swept over $\{32\text{k}, 64\text{k}, 128\text{k}, 256\text{k}\}$ at 400M and the best extrapolated to 3B.
  2. *Control B (the missing control):* the **same hierarchical architecture** as arm 3 — local encoder, global transformer, local decoder — but fed BPE tokens with fixed patch size, isolating architecture from granularity.
  3. *Treatment:* byte-level with dynamic chunking, mean patch size tuned so decode latency matches arm A within 5%.
- **Deciding number.** $\Delta\mathrm{BPB} = \mathrm{BPB}_{\text{arm 3}} - \mathrm{BPB}_{\text{arm 1}}$ on a held-out multi-domain byte corpus (English, Telugu, Chinese, code, math), at **matched ms/byte**, not matched FLOPs.
  - $\Delta \le -0.005$ bits/byte overall **and** $\le 0$ on English alone → byte-level parity is real and not a multilingual artifact.
  - $-0.005 < \Delta < 0.005$ → parity claims are within noise; the case for bytes rests on robustness and equity, not loss.
  - $\Delta \ge 0.005$ with arm 2 $\approx$ arm 3 → the reported gains are architectural, and the tokenizer was never the problem.

Arm 2 is the point of the design. No published study runs it.

## 10. Worked Example

Take 1 MB of held-out text: 500 KB English, 500 KB Telugu.

With a 128k Llama-3-class vocabulary, measure $\rho$ directly: English $\approx 4.4$ bytes/token, Telugu $\approx 1.3$ bytes/token (Telugu characters are 3 bytes in UTF-8 and mostly fall back to near-byte pieces). Token counts:

```
English : 500,000 / 4.4  ≈ 113,600 tokens
Telugu  : 500,000 / 1.3  ≈ 384,600 tokens
Total   :                 ≈ 498,200 tokens   → ρ_corpus ≈ 2.01
```

Suppose the BPE model reports mean token NLL of $2.10$ nats English, $1.05$ nats Telugu.

$$\mathrm{BPB}_{\text{En}} = \frac{2.10}{4.4\ln 2} = 0.688, \qquad \mathrm{BPB}_{\text{Te}} = \frac{1.05}{1.3 \ln 2} = 1.165.$$

Now the byte arm with mean patch size $p = 6.0$ on English and $p = 3.1$ on Telugu (an entropy patcher chunks the lower-entropy Telugu byte stream more aggressively than BPE splits it). Suppose it reports $\mathrm{BPB}_{\text{En}} = 0.694$ and $\mathrm{BPB}_{\text{Te}} = 1.081$.

Corpus BPB is byte-weighted, so each language gets weight $1/2$:

```
BPE   : 0.5(0.688) + 0.5(1.165) = 0.9265
Byte  : 0.5(0.694) + 0.5(1.081) = 0.8875
Δ     : −0.039 bits/byte        → byte model "wins"
```

**Where the obstruction becomes visible.** The $-0.039$ is almost entirely Telugu ($-0.084$); English is $+0.006$, a loss. Change the eval mixture to 90% English and the sign flips to $+0.003$. The headline number is a property of the *eval mixture*, not of the architecture — and no two papers use the same mixture.

Second, the cost check. Global-module steps: BPE $498{,}200$; byte $500{,}000/6.0 + 500{,}000/3.1 = 83{,}300 + 161{,}300 = 244{,}600$ — a 2.0$\times$ reduction in global-module FLOPs, which is the headline efficiency claim. But the byte arm also runs a local byte-rate encoder and decoder over all $1{,}000{,}000$ bytes, plus a boundary predictor that is serial at decode. If the local modules are 10% of $P_{\text{global}}$ each, byte-rate work adds $0.2 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 200{,}000$ global-equivalent steps, cutting the 2.0$\times$ FLOP advantage to roughly $1.1\times$ — and the serial boundary step can erase what remains in wall-clock. FLOP parity and latency parity give opposite verdicts on the same pair of runs. That is the problem.
